The imaging spectrometer was designed to accommodate a bandwidth of 760–840
nm as specified by the project supervisor, Dr Benjamin Greenwood, based on 
the expected operating bandwidth of the laser system.
The imaging spectrometer design consists of a 4f imaging system with a 
diffraction grating inserted in the common Fourier plane (see Figures 
\ref{fig:layout} and \ref{fig:real_layout}). The collimating and focussing
optics were selected as lenses rather than spherical mirrors. This
is because the use of a spherical mirror at a large angle of incidence
produces off-axis astigmatism \cite{kingslake2010}. This can be mitigated by
introducing tight-folds in the optical layout, but this is practically 
challenging due to having to mount components extremely close to eachother.
A drawback of using lenses is that they are prone to chromatic aberration 
\cite{kingslake2010}. This is particularly detrimental as chromatic aberration
is a spatio-spectral coupling and can systematically bias measurements 
towards larger spatial dispersions. Achromatic doublet lenses were used
in an attempt to minimise these effects. The focal lengths of the collimating
and focussing lenses 
were chosen to be 150 mm (Thorlabs AC254-150-B-ML) and 100 mm (Edmund 
Optics \#9823) 
respectively as a middle ground between
maximising the accuracy, and maximising the precision of spatial dispersion
measurements (see Section \ref{sec:pipeline-focal-length-tradeoff}). 

A transmission grating was chosen over a reflection grating for the sake of
avoiding an impractical tight fold in the optical layout. Specifically, a 
volume phase holographic transmission grating was used because of its 
low polarisation dependence and low stray light \cite{thorlabsvph2026}, 
and $\approx 90-95\%$ first-order diffraction 
efficiency at the $14.6^\circ$ design angle-of-incidence across the 760-840
nm bandwidth \cite{thorlabsgp1006l2026}.
